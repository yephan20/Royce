WHICH SEEDS & GRAINS ARE THE BEST?

January 19, 2026

By Cat Pedrick-Graybeck

SHARE THIS POST

Seeds and grains are some of the most versatile ingredients you can add to your diet. From oatmeal and quinoa to chia seeds and flaxseeds, these small but mighty foods provide a variety of nutrients that support a balanced diet. They can add texture to your meals, boost fiber intake, and provide essential vitamins, minerals, healthy fats, and plant-based protein.

But which seeds and grains are the best? The answer depends on what you are looking for. Some are particularly rich in fiber, others provide more protein, and some are excellent sources of healthy fats. Whole grains, such as oats, brown rice, and barley, retain more of their natural nutrients than refined grains. Seeds, including chia, flax, pumpkin, and sunflower seeds, offer their own unique nutritional benefits.

t's also worth remembering that no single seed or grain is perfect. Eating a variety is generally the best way to benefit from their different nutrients. Whether you're looking to support digestive health, add more plant-based protein to your meals, or simply make breakfast more interesting, here are some of the best seeds and grains to keep in your pantry.

The Best Grains to Add to Your Diet

Oats: Oats are a great source of soluble fiber, particularly beta-glucan, which can help lower LDL cholesterol as part of a heart-healthy diet. They're an easy choice for breakfast and work well in overnight oats, homemade granola, and baked goods.

Quinoa: Although often treated like a grain, quinoa is technically a seed. It's naturally gluten-free and provides all nine essential amino acids, making it a complete protein. Quinoa is also a good source of fiber, magnesium, and iron. Use it in grain bowls, salads, or as a substitute for rice.

Brown Rice: Unlike white rice, brown rice retains its bran and germ, providing more fiber and certain vitamins and minerals. Its mild flavor makes it a versatile side dish for vegetables, fish, chicken, and other proteins.

Barley: Barley is particularly rich in beta-glucan, a soluble fiber associated with improved cholesterol levels. Its chewy texture makes it a satisfying addition to soups, stews, and grain salads. Most barley contains gluten, so it isn't suitable for people who need to avoid gluten.

Buckwheat: Despite its name, buckwheat is not a type of wheat. It's a naturally gluten-free seed that provides fiber, minerals, and plant-based protein. Buckwheat flour is commonly used to make pancakes, noodles, and traditional Russian blini.

Farro: This ancient wheat grain has a pleasantly chewy texture and a nutty flavor. It provides fiber, protein, and several B vitamins and minerals. Farro works particularly well in warm salads, soups, and hearty grain bowls. Because it is a type of wheat, it contains gluten.

Millet: Millet is a small, naturally gluten-free grain that provides carbohydrates, fiber, and minerals. It has a mild, slightly nutty flavor and can be cooked as a breakfast porridge, served as a side dish, or added to soups and salads.

The Best Seeds for Nutrition

Chia Seeds: These tiny seeds are packed with fiber and provide plant-based omega-3 fatty acids in the form of ALA (alpha-linolenic acid). When mixed with liquid, they form a gel-like consistency, making them ideal for chia pudding, smoothies, and overnight oats.

Flaxseeds: Flaxseeds are another excellent source of ALA omega-3 fatty acids and fiber. They also contain lignans, plant compounds studied for their potential health benefits. Ground flaxseed is generally easier to digest than whole flaxseed because the body can access more of its nutrients.

Pumpkin Seeds: Also known as pepitas, pumpkin seeds provide plant-based protein, magnesium, zinc, and healthy unsaturated fats. Sprinkle them over salads, roasted vegetables, oatmeal, or yogurt for extra crunch.

Sunflower Seeds: Sunflower seeds are rich in vitamin E, an antioxidant that helps protect cells from damage. They also contain healthy fats, protein, and minerals. Enjoy them as a snack or add them to salads, homemade trail mix, and baked goods.

Sesame Seeds: Sesame seeds contain healthy fats, fiber, calcium, and other minerals. Their nutritional composition varies depending on whether they are hulled or unhulled. Tahini, a paste made from sesame seeds, is a versatile ingredient in dressings, dips, and sauces.

Hemp Seeds: THemp hearts are a convenient source of plant-based protein and unsaturated fats. They provide all nine essential amino acids, although the amount of protein in a typical serving is smaller than in many concentrated protein sources. Their mild, nutty flavor makes them easy to add to smoothies, oatmeal, salads, and yogurt.

Poppy Seeds: These tiny seeds contribute fiber, unsaturated fats, and minerals, including calcium and manganese. They're often used in breads, muffins, salad dressings, and other baked goods.

How to Incorporate More Seeds and Grains Into Your Meals

1. Start with breakfast. Add chia seeds, ground flaxseed, or pumpkin seeds to oatmeal, yogurt, or smoothies. You can also make overnight oats with milk or a fortified plant-based alternative.

2. Swap refined grains for whole grains. Try brown rice instead of white rice, whole-grain bread instead of refined bread, or quinoa in place of a portion of your usual side dish.

3. Add crunch to salads. Sprinkle sunflower seeds, sesame seeds, or pumpkin seeds over leafy greens, roasted vegetables, and grain bowls.

4. Make your own trail mix. Combine pumpkin seeds, sunflower seeds, oats, and unsweetened dried fruit for a convenient snack. Add nuts if you enjoy them and have no relevant allergies.

5. Experiment with baking. Ground flaxseed, chia seeds, and whole-grain flours can add texture and nutrients to muffins, breads, and pancakes. Keep in mind that changing flour types can affect the texture and moisture of a recipe.

6. Try different grains in soups and side dishes. Barley, millet, farro, and quinoa can add variety to meals that typically rely on pasta or rice.
